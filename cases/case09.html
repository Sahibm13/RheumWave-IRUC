A 44-year-old male construction worker with longstanding psoriasis, controlled with topical therapy, presents with a 2-year history of widespread DIP pain.

He describes constant pain, stiffness in the mornings and difficulty making a fist first thing, but he cannot quantify for how long. It worsens with using his hands for his job and can hurt more in the evenings on days he works. There is swelling at the DIPs which worsens when he uses his hands a lot. There is mild benefit with over-the-counter NSAIDs. No other joints are painful or swollen. No dactylitis or enthesitis symptoms. He has no inflammatory back pain. Review of systems is otherwise negative.

He is otherwise healthy except for a meniscal injury in his 20s. Family history is non-contributory. He drinks 2–3 beers a week and has no smoking history.

Examination reveals subtle swelling and bogginess at several DIP joints, which were tender. The rest of the MSK exam is normal. He has mild nail pitting on both hands and <1% BSA of psoriasis.

X-rays were done externally and reported as normal (you do not have the images). Bloodwork shows HLA-B27 positivity and a CRP of 4 (normal). The rest of the investigations are normal.

You decide to do a bedside MSK ultrasound of the DIP joints.

The images show a small enthesophyte — a bony proliferation forming at the enthesis and arising from the cortex, in this case at the insertion of the extensor tendon. There can be tendon thickening and hypoechogenicity with enthesitis, and Doppler may be present.

Osteophytes can look very similar and are well-defined step-up bony projections that occur more at the joint margins. The two can coexist, but they will be in different locations with different characteristics.

Osteophyte Enthesophyte
Hyperechoic bony overgrowth Ossification within a tendon or ligament
Intra-articular or peri-articular location Located at entheses
Linear orientation of the bony proliferation, parallel to the tendon, due to the direction of pull

Diagnosing enthesitis versus osteoarthritis at the small joints of the hands (PIP/DIP) can be very challenging. We would recommend scanning patients with well-established diagnoses and clear findings of OA and of small joint enthesitis, to build confidence in differentiating the two and avoid erroneously overcalling.

Q2 Which of the following best describes the finding?
Flexor tenosynovitis
Flexor peritenonitis
Synovitis
Flexor insertion enthesitis

The image shows flexor tenosynovitis at the level of the PIP joint. Flexor tenosynovitis is hypoechoic or anechoic thickened tissue, with or without fluid, within the tendon sheath, seen in two perpendicular planes, and which may exhibit intra-tenosynovial power Doppler signal.

It is associated more with inflammatory etiologies (RA, PsA, crystal flares, infectious tenosynovitis). It can also be seen in stenosing or overuse tenosynovitis (trigger finger). It is uncommon in OA — if you see flexor tenosynovitis, especially in multiple fingers, your suspicion of inflammatory pathology should be higher.

Peritenonitis describes hypoechoic swelling surrounding the extensor tendons at the fingers, with or without power Doppler signal (the tendon itself may be normal or thickened). Because the finger extensor tendons do not have a true tendon synovial sheath, the term peritenonitis is used. We cannot call this peritenonitis, as the flexor tendons do have a synovial sheath.

While there may be synovitis here, the predominant finding is tenosynovitis. The pathology is along the course of the tendon sheath and not at an insertional point (enthesis), so this is not flexor insertion enthesitis.

Q3 When you are writing your ultrasound report in your note, how will you grade the grey-scale and power Doppler tenosynovitis?

There is a well-established semi-quantitative OMERACT scoring system for tenosynovitis (Naredo E et al. Ann Rheum Dis. 2013).

Grey Scale Power Doppler
0 — normal 0 — no Doppler
1 — minimal hypoechoic/anechoic thickened tissue ± fluid within the tendon sheath 1 — focal intra-tenosynovial Doppler activity
2 — moderate thickening ± fluid distending the sheath 2 — multifocal intra-tenosynovial Doppler activity
3 — severe thickening ± fluid with marked distension of the sheath 3 — diffuse intra-tenosynovial Doppler activity

For easy recall, we will often teach the following:

  • Grey scale: none → mild → moderate → severe sheath thickening
  • Power Doppler: none → focal → multifocal → diffuse vascularity

The grey-scale findings show significant thickening with marked distension of the sheath (one could argue this could be grade 2, but most of us would say grade 3 for GS). The Doppler signal is clearly diffuse and intra-tenosynovial rather than multifocal, and would therefore be grade 3. It would be reported as tenosynovitis at the 3rd right finger (GS gr 3, PD gr 3).

The DUET Study by GRAPPA (grappanetwork.org/duet-study) and the subsequent DUET scoring system (Eder L et al. Arthritis Rheumatol. 2025; 77 (suppl 9)) aimed to develop and validate a novel sonographic scoring system for enthesitis to aid in PsA diagnosis, particularly among individuals with tender entheseal sites. The findings, however, suggest that large joint enthesitis is not a universal feature of early PsA and that false positives may occur.

While external validation and its utility in early PsA diagnosis are still pending, it is a quick and useful screen to check for large joint enthesitis. This extra information can add to your clinical, serological and radiographic assessment of the patient to aid in diagnosing PsA.

The sites scanned to use the tool are the triceps insertion into the olecranon, the patellar tendon insertion into the patella, and the Achilles insertion into the calcaneus. Summary: DIP pain in a patient with psoriasis and nail pitting sits at the crossroads of osteoarthritis and psoriatic arthritis, and the small joints are where sonographic discrimination is hardest. A bony proliferation arising from the cortex at the extensor insertion, oriented along the line of pull, is an enthesophyte; a step-up projection at the joint margin is an osteophyte, and the two can coexist. When the entheseal appearance is equivocal, scanning a second territory changes the picture — flexor tenosynovitis in multiple fingers is uncommon in OA and shifts suspicion firmly toward inflammatory disease. Report what you see using the OMERACT semi-quantitative scale, grading grey-scale and power Doppler separately (here, GS gr 3 and PD gr 3). Finally, the DUET sites — triceps, patellar and Achilles insertions — offer a quick large-joint entheseal screen that adds to, but does not replace, the clinical, serological and radiographic assessment.

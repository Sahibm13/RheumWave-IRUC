A 48-year-old female with psoriasis over the last year (BSA 2%) is referred by dermatology for assessment of intermittent arthralgias over the last year. Her psoriasis is treated with topicals only.

She describes intermittent episodes lasting 1-2 days of migratory pain affecting wrists, fingers, feet and Achilles/ankle. She has morning stiffness for 30 minutes but also describes pain more after use or activity and at the end of the day. She does not endorse any overt clinical swelling or dactylitis. She has no back pain. No history of uveitis or IBD.

On exam, her vitals are normal and BMI is elevated at 29. She has no swollen joints or dactylitis. There is mild tenderness over the wrists and right ankle but no swelling appreciated. She is also tender at the right Achilles insertion but no swelling. No other swollen or tender entheseal sites.

BW showed HLA-B27 positivity and CRP of 7.2 mg/L (N<5 mg/L). X-rays were reported normal.

You decide to do a focused bedside MSK ultrasound to assess for subclinical synovitis and enthesitis.

Bedside Ultrasound — Wrist

New Information

Q1 You scan the wrist and see the images above. Which of the following is the most accurate interpretation?
Tenosynovitis of extensor tendon of wrist
Erosion at the capitate
Normal
Effusion at the radiocarpal joint

There is no tenosynovitis of the extensor tendon. The hypoechoic area in the middle image is the extensor retinaculum that is often confused with tenosynovitis. On transverse there is mild anisotropy as the tendon is not hyperechoic. You can differentiate pathology from artifact by tilting the probe to see if the tendon changes from hyperechoic to hypoechoic, which would suggest artifact as pathology would not change. Tenosynovitis would be seen as hypoechoic to anechoic (if fluid is also present) surrounding the tendon with Doppler seen in two planes (long and transverse).

There is mild irregularity at the capitate but no clear cortical break seen in two planes to suggest erosion. This can be seen in normal individuals too.

The mild hypoechoic areas at the radiocarpal and midcarpal articulations can be seen in normal individuals as that is at the synovial recesses of these joints. There is no clear synovitis or synovial hypertrophy in GS and no Doppler to suggest pathology.

Bedside Ultrasound — Finger Joints

New Information

Q2 The finger joints show the images above. Which of the following is the most accurate interpretation?
Normal MCP and normal flexor tendon
Anisotropy of the flexor tendon and normal MCP
Tenosynovitis of the flexor tendon and normal MCP
Tenosynovitis of the flexor tendon with effusion of MCP joint

The MCP joint is normal. Even if one were to suspect a bit more hypoechoic echotexture in the synovium in long GS images, there is no PD in the region and no GS or PD synovitis is seen in transverse images. Pathology requires confirmation in two planes, which is not seen here. These MCP images were obtained in an individual with normal joints using a handheld device, and it is not uncommon to see slight echotexture variations. It is important to get used to normal variations between individuals but also with your machine.

The flexor tendon is hypoechoic to anechoic on the right side of the image (distally). This is because of anisotropy as the tendon dips deeper and this results in the ultrasound beam not hitting the tendon in a perpendicular manner and being deflected away from the probe. Anisotropy is often confused with tenosynovitis and results in erroneous interpretation of pathology. You can differentiate by moving your probe (heel-toe, tilting) to differentiate pathology (no change to image) from artifact (should go hyperechoic to hypoechoic).

Bedside Ultrasound — Right Ankle (Tibiotalar)

New Information

The left ankle, which was non-tender, showed a normal ultrasound. The right ankle (tibiotalar) scan showed an anechoic region at the distal tibiotalar joint. There was no Doppler signal (images not available). The subtalar joint, anterior, medial and lateral tendons were normal.

Q3 Which of the following is the most correct interpretation?
Gr 1 synovitis in grey-scale without PD signal
Gr 2 synovitis in grey-scale without PD signal
Moderate-sized pathological effusion
Normal physiological effusion

This is a normal joint with physiologic effusion, seen not uncommonly in individuals at the distal tibiotalar joint. This is not synovitis as it is anechoic, which suggests effusion or fluid. There is no Doppler either. The effusion size is small and physiologic in this case.

This can be seen in individuals with degenerative pathology or elevated BMI as well. To differentiate normal physiologic from pathology, look for other areas in the ankle and also in clinical context.

Bedside Ultrasound — Achilles Tendons (Longitudinal)

New Information

The right Achilles was tender on palpation at insertion.

Q4 The Achilles longitudinal scans show the images above. What is the most appropriate interpretation?
Normal
Traction enthesophyte
Inflammatory enthesitis
Mechanical tendonitis

Diagnosing inflammatory enthesitis sonographically comprises inflammatory changes (hypoechogenicity, thickening, Doppler) and structural changes (erosions, enthesophytes and calcifications) which have to be close to or at the entheses. The definition of “close to” is debated between OMERACT (within 2mm) and GRAPPA (“near the bone cortex”) definitions. The diagnosis is dependent on a combination of these factors with Doppler and erosions being shown to be most specific. The definition of inflammatory enthesitis at the single enthesis level requires the presence of a power Doppler signal grade ≥2 near the bone cortex, in the presence of hypoechogenicity and/or thickening of the entheseal structure, and within the appropriate clinical context (Ribeiro A et al. Arthritis Rheumatol. 2025). A DUET score has also been developed to help in the diagnosis of inflammatory enthesitis at the patient level, although its external validation is pending (Eder L et al. Arthritis Rheumatol. 2025).

In this case, the left Achilles is completely normal as it has none of the aforementioned abnormalities. The right Achilles shows a moderate-sized traction enthesophyte. There could be hypoechogenicity at the distal aspect of the tendon but in reality that was anisotropy as dynamic testing showed it becoming hyperechoic. However, there is no thickening, Doppler, erosions or calcifications that would be seen in inflammatory enthesitis. Therefore, even if hypoechogenicity were present, it would not meet the definition for inflammatory enthesitis at single level mentioned above.

Tendonitis may look sonographically very similar to inflammatory enthesitis with thickening, hypoechogenicity, Doppler and calcifications. However, many times this involves a significant proportion of the proximal tendon or is isolated proximally with no distal entheseal involvement. It would also be unusual to see erosions with mechanical tendonitis, so if erosions are present, this should make you suspect inflammatory enthesitis.

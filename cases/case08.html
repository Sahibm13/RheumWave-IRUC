Case 8

Core Foot/Ankle Enthesitis
Question 1 of 6

Case Vignette

48-year-old woman with longstanding psoriasis, referred for assessment of psoriatic arthritis because of bilateral posterior ankle pain. (Expand for full history, exam & investigations.)

A 48-year-old accountant with longstanding psoriasis is referred to your clinic for assessment of psoriatic arthritis because of "ankle pain." Her pain is in the posterior aspect of the ankle, by the Achilles, bilaterally. It hurts a little at rest but worsens with use, such as walking longer distances and hiking. She notices intermittent subjective swelling, more so at the left Achilles. She has not noticed swelling at the anterior ankle, midfoot or forefoot, and no other joints are bothersome.

The pain responds to acetaminophen (mild benefit) and over-the-counter NSAIDs (moderate benefit). She has a family history of psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis. She neither smokes nor drinks. She has pre-diabetes and borderline elevated lipids but is not currently on medication for either. Her only medication is guselkumab (an IL-23 inhibitor) for her psoriasis, through her dermatologist.

On examination her BMI is 30. She has no active psoriasis and no nail changes. No joints are swollen today and there is no dactylitis. She is tender at both Achilles tendons, and there is a small bump at the left Achilles distally, just before its insertion into the calcaneus. No other entheses are tender. Axial examination is normal.

X-rays comment on bilateral Achilles enthesophytes and are otherwise normal. She is HLA-B27 positive. Her CRP is 15 mg/L (normal <10).

You decide to do a point-of-care ultrasound of the Achilles and to screen for enthesitis.

Enthesophyte, calcification and hypoechogenicity
The video shows a large enthesophyte, with potentially a smaller linear one as well. Both rise from the calcaneus and are therefore enthesophytes, not calcification. An enthesophyte is a step-up bony prominence at the normal bone contour, and ideally should be confirmed in both longitudinal and transverse planes. A calcification, by contrast, is a hyperechoic linear structure detected within the tendon or ligament near the insertion to the bone, but with no congruency with the bone.

There is no thickening or hypoechogenicity — the fibrillar structure of the tendon is seen well, with no hypoechoic areas. There is no erosion. What looks like a "cortical break" is actually an acoustic shadow below the enthesophyte, shown below. No Doppler signal is seen within the tendon or at the entheses.

Enthesophytes are not specific, and can be seen in many individuals with purely mechanical causes for entheseal pain. One should not diagnose "enthesitis" on the basis of an enthesophyte alone. For active inflammatory enthesitis, one needs to see thickening and/or hypoechogenicity with at least grade 2 power Doppler, in the absence of another explanation (Ribeiro A. et al., Arthritis Rheumatol. 2025).

That is a more specific definition, designed for trials. In clinical practice, if there has been inflammatory enthesitis that has since "burnt out," Doppler may be absent but you would see other changes such as thickening, hypoechogenicity, enthesophytes, calcifications or erosions. The more elementary findings there are, the more confident you can be. The presence of erosions in particular should make you more suspicious of inflammatory enthesitis rather than mechanical entheseal change.

Be careful with formal ultrasound reports that comment on the presence of enthesitis or enthesopathy. Many will use the term on the basis of an enthesophyte alone, without other inflammatory findings.

When thinking about what to look for to identify inflammatory enthesitis on ultrasound, try to hold in mind three inflammatory and three structural elementary findings.

Inflammatory
  • Thickening — increased thickness of the tendon or ligament at the enthesis compared with its body, and/or a concave appearance of the tendon at the insertion site. Thickness can be difficult to judge, and should be suspected when accompanied by other entheseal lesions such as loss of fibrillary echotexture.
  • Hypoechogenicity — distinct loss of homogeneous fibrillar echotexture, with relative hypoechogenicity compared with the rest of the enthesis, after correcting for anisotropy.
  • Power Doppler — positive Doppler signal at the enthesis, confirmed in two perpendicular planes and distinguished from surface reflection artifact and nutritional vessel signal. Depending on the definition used (OMERACT vs GRAPPA), there is some debate as to whether it must be within 2 mm of the enthesis or may be proximal to that.
Structural
  • Erosions — a cortical defect confirmed with a step-down contour defect, detected in two planes, at the insertion of the tendon or ligament to the bone.
  • Enthesophyte — a step-up bony prominence at the normal bone contour. Severity is graded on length; readers are not expected to measure it, but suggested cut-off points exist to guide grading.
  • Calcifications — hyperechoic linear structures detected within the tendon or ligament at the insertion to the bone, but with no congruency with the bone.
This video shows several inflammatory findings, including thickening of the Achilles — it does not taper into the calcaneal insertion — and hypoechogenicity, with loss of the fibrillar structure of the tendon in several areas on grey-scale. There are enthesophytes at the calcaneus.

There are no erosions; the gaps you see in the cortex are acoustic shadowing, as in Q1. There are no calcifications, as all the bony prominences are connected to the calcaneal cortex. The power Doppler is outside the tendon, and certainly not at the entheseal region.

Left Achilles — Your Patient

Q3 Your patient from the vignette has these ultrasound findings at the left Achilles in isolation, without any changes at other entheses. Would this be enough to diagnose psoriatic arthritis?
Yes
No
The video shows the same left Achilles as Q2, with thickening, hypoechogenicity and enthesophytes. While these findings can certainly be seen in inflammatory enthesitis with PsA or SpA, similar changes can be seen in moderate to severe mechanical enthesopathy, especially in the lower extremities at the Achilles. Sonography alone cannot differentiate them, and ultrasound alone should not be used to make a diagnosis of PsA or SpA.

Some pointers to consider:
  • The clinical context is critical. She has psoriasis, is HLA-B27 positive, and her CRP is mildly elevated. All of these may be explained by PsA and inflammatory enthesitis, but none is specific — her CRP may be up because of her weight and BMI, and HLA-B27 is present in around 10% of the population and in people with psoriasis. She is someone in whom we would screen other entheseal sites, monitor for evolution, and treat the local Achilles pathology. We would also look for correctable mechanical causes of Achilles tendonitis or enthesopathy, such as foot deformities like pes planus, or valgus and varus leg deformity at the knee.
  • The more findings there are, structural and inflammatory, the more confidence there is that this is inflammatory enthesitis. The DUET study investigators found that experts were more confident with ≥4 elementary lesions, and more uncertain with 2–4 lesions, as in this case (Ribeiro A. et al., Arthritis Rheumatol. 2025).
  • Power Doppler grade 2–3 and/or erosions were the findings that most increased the DUET investigators' confidence that a picture was inflammatory rather than otherwise. This patient's ultrasound showed neither.
The clinician should therefore be cautious about labeling this definite inflammatory enthesitis from PsA, with only three findings, without erosions or power Doppler, and with potential alternative explanations from the clinical presentation. Given the clinical context and the ultrasound findings, however, one should screen other areas for enthesitis, complete the other investigations for PsA, and monitor the patient for progression or evolution.

The DUET study by GRAPPA (grappanetwork.org/duet-study) and the subsequent DUET scoring system (Eder L. et al., Arthritis Rheumatol. 2025; 77 (suppl 9)) aimed to develop and validate a novel sonographic scoring system for enthesitis to aid in PsA diagnosis, particularly among individuals with tender entheseal sites. The findings suggest that large joint enthesitis is not a universal feature of early PsA, and that false positives may occur.

While external validation and its utility in early PsA diagnosis are still pending, it is a quick and useful screen for large joint enthesitis. That extra information can add to your clinical, serological and radiographic assessment of the patient.

The sites to scan are the triceps insertion into the olecranon, the patellar tendon insertion into the patella, and the Achilles insertion into the calcaneus. At each you look for inflammatory signs (thickening, hypoechogenicity, Doppler) and structural changes (erosions, enthesophytes) to complete the DUET score. This triceps image shows thickening of the tendon, hypoechogenicity with loss of the fibrillar echotexture, and power Doppler at the site of hypoechogenicity, close to the enthesis. There is a large enthesophyte, but no calcification and no erosions.

These findings therefore meet the single-enthesis definition mentioned earlier: the presence of a power Doppler signal grade ≥2 near the bone cortex, in the presence of hypoechogenicity and/or thickening of the entheseal structure, and within the appropriate clinical context (Ribeiro A. et al., Arthritis Rheumatol. 2025).

There are also four elementary lesions with power Doppler, which makes you more certain of inflammatory enthesitis than the earlier Achilles findings, which had only three lesions and neither Doppler nor erosions. The triceps is also less of a loading site for mechanical change, which allows a little more confidence.

Your score for this case
Summary: An enthesophyte is not enthesitis. It is a step-up bony prominence congruent with the cortex, it is common in people with purely mechanical entheseal pain, and formal reports frequently use the word "enthesopathy" on the strength of one. Active inflammatory enthesitis asks for more: three inflammatory findings — thickening, hypoechogenicity, power Doppler — alongside three structural ones — erosions, enthesophytes, calcifications. Watch for the traps: a gap in the cortex beneath an enthesophyte is acoustic shadowing, not an erosion, and Doppler outside the tendon is not entheseal Doppler. The Achilles in this case shows three lesions without Doppler or erosion, and at a heavily loaded site where mechanical enthesopathy looks much the same — which is why the scan alone cannot make the diagnosis. The triceps changes it: four lesions including grade ≥2 Doppler, at a site far less exposed to mechanical loading. That is the value of the DUET screen — triceps, patellar insertion, Achilles — not one enthesis read in isolation, but the pattern across several, read alongside the clinical picture.
